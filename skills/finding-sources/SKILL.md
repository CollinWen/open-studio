# Finding sources worth studying

You are choosing what someone will spend real attention on. A short list of
sources they learn from beats a long list they skim. Most of what a search
returns fails; that's expected. Say so and keep looking rather than padding.

## Where to look

Go toward the people who made the thing, not the people who wrote about it.

- **The original**: the paper, the maker's own site or channel, the book, the
  repository, the recipe from the cook who developed it, the artist's statement.
- **Practitioners' writing**: long posts, talks, workshop notes, process
  videos, forum threads where experts argue in detail.
- **Where taste is kept**: museum and gallery pages, university course pages,
  curated archives, respected small publishers, the bibliography of a source
  that already passed.
- **Follow the chain**: who a good source cites, thanks or answers, and who
  later cites it. One strong source usually leads to the next two.

Treat as leads at best: listicles, SEO roundups, content farms, reposts without
credit, AI-written summaries, aggregators, anything selling the thing it
reviews. If a repost is all you can find, find the original before adding it.

For a reel, short or post you can't fully read, use the caption, the creator's
name and the visible work to find their own site, longer pieces, or the source
they drew on. Add the fuller source and mention the reel in the description.

## The four tests

Every source must pass all four. Look for evidence, not vibes, and be ready to
say in one sentence why it passed.

### 1. Credibility: does the maker have provable standing?

- Pass: a track record you can check (their body of work, a role, publications,
  years of practice shown in what they've made, peers who cite or credit them).
- Pass, too: an unknown maker whose work itself proves deep knowledge (a
  hobbyist folder whose crease patterns are rigorous, a home cook who tests
  methods and shows the failures).
- Fail: no traceable author, borrowed authority ("experts say"), credentials
  unrelated to the subject, claims that can't be checked.

### 2. Novelty: does it say something new, thought through?

- Pass: a new approach, a perspective you haven't seen elsewhere, an
  argument that reframes the subject, a technique worked out from first principles.
- Fail: a restatement of the standard view, a summary of other summaries,
  "10 tips" with nothing behind them. Old is fine; derivative isn't. The
  origin of a well-known idea passes; the hundredth retelling doesn't.

### 3. Execution: is it made with care?

- Pass: attention to detail, nuance, intricacy; the message lands; you can
  tell real time went into how it is delivered (clear diagrams, precise
  writing, a well-shot process, clean and documented code, a recipe with
  weights and reasons).
- Fail: sloppy, padded, vague where it matters, stops right before the hard part.

### 4. Value to the owner: is it worth *their* time?

- Read the board's brief, the starting entry and its chain first; you are
  choosing for this person, not for a general audience.
- Pass: it fits what they're working on, or brings a perspective they should
  consider, draws a parallel they hadn't made, has an interesting background
  story, and leaves room to explore further rather than closing the subject.
- Ask: would this be learning material for someone like them? Would they come
  back to it?
- Fail: correct but already known to them (check the studio first), too basic,
  exhaustive in a way that leaves nothing to think about, or off their path.

## Recording what you decide

- **Added**: one entry per source, at its original link, with `builds_on` set.
  The description says what it is, what to take from it, and in one sentence
  why it passed ("A structural engineer's 40-page derivation of curved-crease
  stiffness, with physical tests; the only source that measures it").
- **Turned down**: log a `finding` for strong candidates you rejected and the
  test they failed ("Passed on X: well made, but restates Y, which is already
  on the board"). This is how the owner checks your judgement.
- **Unsure**: log a `question` rather than adding it.
- **Reading order**: end your run's summary with the order you'd read the
  finds in, and why.

## How many

Three excellent sources beat ten adequate ones. If you can't find any that
pass, say so in your summary with what you searched; that is a useful result.
