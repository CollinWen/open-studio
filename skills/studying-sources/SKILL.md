# Studying a source

Finding a good source is half the job. The other half is digestion: short
pieces slip away because nothing is done with them. What makes knowledge stick
is retrieval, generation and connection. Summaries work against this: they
create a fluency illusion, where recognising something feels like
understanding it (Andy Matuschak, "Why books don't work"). So your job is to
make the owner's reading more active, never to read for them.

## 1. Understand it yourself first

Read or watch the whole thing. Then, in your own notes:

- **The argument in three sentences**: the problem, the move, the result.
- **The claims**, each as something that could be wrong, with the passage it
  comes from. Separate what the source shows (data, a worked case, a proof)
  from what it only asserts.
- **The worked example** at its core. Rebuild it: if you can't redo the
  author's central case step by step, you don't understand it yet.
- **The edges**: where the author says it fails, and where you think it fails
  that they didn't say.
- **The names and works it rests on**: who it cites, answers or borrows from.
  These are leads for `gathering-sources`.

Test yourself the way Feynman did: hold a concrete case in your head and check
each claim against it.

## 2. Connect it to the studio

- `search_studio` and `find_related` for what it touches on the board. Read
  those entries and their chains.
- Find where it **builds on**, answers or contradicts what's there. Link your
  entry with `builds_on` and a note on how ("the experiment behind",
  "disagrees with").
- Note the parallel nobody has drawn yet. That's often the most valuable line
  you write.

## 3. Hand it over so the owner thinks

In the entry's description, use the hand-over from `gathering-sources` (what
it is, the open problem it hits, the claim worth testing, a question to hold
while reading). Then help their reading become active:

- **Before reading: a prediction.** End the description with a prompt for them
  to guess the argument before they open it ("Before reading: how do you
  think the author gets stiffness from a curved crease?").
- **After reading: their own words.** In the log, a `question` asking for a few
  sentences in their own words, or what they'd test first. The owner answers
  by writing a note entry that builds on the source.
- **Link their note.** When the owner writes such a note, suggest in a
  `thought` which earlier notes it connects to, Zettelkasten-style. (You can
  link only agents' unpublished entries yourself; the owner makes the rest.)
- **Resurface their notes, not the source.** When a later find touches the same
  idea, quote the owner's own note in your log line, not the original source:
  their words are what they'll remember.

## 4. Keep their taste in training

Taste develops by making judgments. If you always judge, the owner's own
taste weakens. So now and then (about one pick in five, or when the brief asks
for it), add a source **blind**: describe what it is and nothing about why you
picked it, start the description with "Blind pick: judge it yourself first",
and log your scores and quotes as a `finding` the owner can open after. When
they've published or set it aside, compare in a `thought`.

## 5. Write up

On a study run, log a `finding` with the argument, the claims and the edges,
then gather a few related sources (`gathering-sources`, within its budget).
Finish with a summary: the source's argument in three sentences, the one claim
worth testing, and the reading order you'd suggest.
